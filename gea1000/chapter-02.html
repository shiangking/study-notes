Central habit: ask “proportion of what?”

Before calculating or interpreting any proportion, identify its denominator. Most errors in this chapter come from silently switching the population being used.

2. Categorical variables and summaries

A categorical variable places each observation into a category. Examples include treatment type, success or failure, gender, and responses on a Likert scale from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree.” A numerical code used for a category does not automatically make the variable numerical.

One categorical variable

In the kidney-stone dataset, 831 of 1,050 treatments succeeded and 219 failed.

A proportion lies from 0 to 1 inclusive; as a percentage, it lies from 0% to 100% inclusive.

Useful plots

  • Bar plot: compares category counts or proportions.
  • Stacked bar plot: shows how subcategories make up a total.
  • 100% stacked bar plot: normalises every group to 100%, making group compositions directly comparable.

3. Marginal, joint and conditional proportions

3.1 Marginal proportion

A marginal proportion uses a row or column total from the table’s margin and divides it by the grand total.

$\displaystyle P(Y)=\frac{350}{1050}=0.333, \qquad P(\text{Success})=\frac{831}{1050}=0.791$

3.2 Joint proportion

A joint proportion describes two characteristics occurring together and uses the grand total.

$\displaystyle P(Y\cap\text{Failure})=\frac{61}{1050}=0.0581$

3.3 Conditional proportion

A conditional proportion restricts attention to a subgroup. The event after the vertical bar determines the denominator.

$\displaystyle P(\text{Success}\mid X)=\frac{542}{700}=0.774, \qquad P(\text{Success}\mid Y)=\frac{289}{350}=0.826$

Thus, about 77.4% of patients receiving X succeeded, compared with 82.6% of patients receiving Y.

Why counts alone can mislead

Treatment X has more successes than Y: 542 versus 289. However, twice as many patients received X. Comparing the conditional success proportions normalises for the different group sizes and suggests that Y has the higher success rate.

Association is not causation

The kidney data came from an observational study. The higher success proportion for Y shows an association, but it does not prove that Y caused better outcomes. Other variables—such as stone size or how treatments were assigned—may explain the pattern.

4. Study design determines the measure

DesignStarting pointDirectionWhat can be calculated?
Cohort studyExposure statusFollow participants forward to observe outcomesIncidence proportions, RR and OR
Case-control studyOutcome status: cases and controlsLook backward for exposureOR, but not incidence proportions or RR

For a potentially harmful exposure such as vaping, randomly assigning people to vape would be unethical. An observational design is therefore appropriate.

In a case-control study, researchers choose how many cases and controls to include. The observed disease proportion is therefore fixed by the sampling design and does not estimate disease risk in the population.

5. Incidence proportions and risk ratio

A cohort study follows 1,000 vapers and 1,000 non-vapers who initially have no respiratory disease:

GroupDeveloped diseaseDid not develop diseaseTotal
Vaper (exposed)1458551,000
Non-vaper (unexposed)1018991,000

The incidence proportion is the proportion of initially disease-free people who develop the disease during the study period.

$\displaystyle \text{Risk}_{E}=\frac{145}{1000}=0.145, \qquad \text{Risk}_{U}=\frac{101}{1000}=0.101$

Using exposed as the comparison group of interest:

$\displaystyle RR=\frac{\text{Risk in exposed}}{\text{Risk in unexposed}}=\frac{0.145}{0.101}\approx1.44$

Interpretation

Vapers were 1.44 times as likely to develop respiratory disease as non-vapers during the study period. Equivalently, their risk was about 44% higher, because $(1.44-1)\times100\%=44\%$.

ValueDirection of associationInterpretation
$RR=1$No associationBoth groups have the same risk.
$RR>1$Positive associationThe outcome is more likely in the numerator group.
$RR<1$Negative associationThe outcome is less likely in the numerator group.

6. Odds and odds ratio

The odds of an event compare the probability that it occurs with the probability that it does not occur.

$\displaystyle \text{Odds}(A)=\frac{P(A)}{P(A^c)}=\frac{P(A)}{1-P(A)}=\frac{\text{number with }A}{\text{number without }A}$

If Singapore has a 20% chance of winning a match, its odds of winning are $0.20/0.80=0.25$, or $1:4$. Probability and odds are related but are not the same quantity.

Case-control example

$\displaystyle \text{Odds}_{E}=\frac{15}{35}=0.429, \qquad \text{Odds}_{U}=\frac{235}{715}=0.329$
$\displaystyle OR=\frac{\text{odds in exposed}}{\text{odds in unexposed}}=\frac{15/35}{235/715}\approx1.30$

The odds of respiratory disease among vapers are about 1.30 times the odds among non-vapers. This is a positive association. Do not rewrite this as “30% higher risk,” because an OR compares odds, not risks.

8. Simpson’s paradox: overall versus within-group results

Lecture material. Simpson’s paradox occurs when a trend or association appears in every individual subgroup (stratum), but disappears or reverses when the groups are combined. Stratification means splitting the data by a third variable so that comparisons can be made within more comparable groups.

The kidney-stone example studies three categorical variables: treatment (X or Y, the explanatory variable), outcome (success or failure, the response variable), and initial stone size (large or small, the stratifying variable). The lecture is presented by Mr. Edmil CHUE, Office of the Provost, NUS.

9. Why the direction reverses

Lecture material. The treatment groups have very different case mixes. Large stones are the more difficult cases in this example: their pooled success rate is 71.9%, compared with 89.0% for small stones. X receives disproportionately many large-stone cases; Y receives disproportionately many small-stone cases.

TreatmentLarge stonesSmall stonesTotalLarge shareSmall share
X52617470075.1%24.9%
Y8027035022.9%77.1%
Total6064441,05057.7%42.3%

The 75.1% figure means 75.1% of X’s patients had large stones (526/700). It does not mean 75.1% of all large-stone patients received X: that is a different denominator, 526/606.

Supplementary explanation: overall rates are weighted averages

Let S denote success, L large stones and M small stones. For either treatment T:

$P(S\mid T)=P(S\mid T,L)P(L\mid T)+P(S\mid T,M)P(M\mid T)$

Each subgroup’s success rate is multiplied by the fraction of that treatment group belonging to the subgroup.

$P(S\mid X)=\frac{381}{526}\frac{526}{700}+\frac{161}{174}\frac{174}{700}=\frac{542}{700}\approx77.4\%$
$P(S\mid Y)=\frac{55}{80}\frac{80}{350}+\frac{234}{270}\frac{270}{350}=\frac{289}{350}\approx82.6\%$

X’s overall average is pulled towards its lower large-stone rate because about three-quarters of its patients have large stones. Y’s average is pulled towards its higher small-stone rate because about three-quarters have small stones. The averages use different weights, so they can reverse the within-stratum ordering.

Do not simply average the two subgroup percentages: that would assign equal weights even when subgroup sizes are unequal.

Supplementary worked example: compare an identical case mix

Imagine weighting both treatments as 50% large and 50% small, while retaining their observed stratum-specific success rates:

$X:\;0.5(381/526)+0.5(161/174)\approx82.5\%$
$Y:\;0.5(55/80)+0.5(234/270)\approx77.7\%$

X now has the higher rate. These are illustrative standardised rates, not additional observed results. Any common mix of the two strata preserves X’s advantage here because X has the higher rate in each stratum. Standardisation alone does not establish causation.

10. Confounders: definition and verification

Lecture material. A confounder is an extraneous variable associated with both the explanatory variable and the outcome, which can distort the apparent relationship between them. Here, initial stone size is related both to treatment choice and to success.

Criterion 1: stone size is associated with treatment

$P(L\mid X)=526/700\approx75.1\%,\qquad P(L\mid Y)=80/350\approx22.9\%$

The proportions differ substantially. Large stones are more common among X patients than Y patients, so treatment and stone size are associated.

Criterion 2: stone size is associated with outcome

Stone sizeSuccessFailureTotalSuccess rate
Large43617060671.9%
Small3954944489.0%
Total8312191,05079.1%
$P(S\mid L)=436/606\approx71.9\%,\qquad P(S\mid M)=395/444\approx89.0\%$

Large stones are negatively associated with success. Together, these two comparisons support the lecture’s identification of stone size as a confounder: the more difficult cases are concentrated in X.

Supplementary clarification: association checks are not the entire causal argument

A causal interpretation also depends on how and when variables arise. Initial stone size is measured before treatment, making it a plausible factor influencing both treatment choice and success. A variable caused by treatment is not automatically a confounder merely because it is associated with treatment and outcome.

The within-size results support “X has a higher observed success rate for both stone sizes.” They do not, by themselves, justify “X causes better outcomes”: other confounders may remain.

11. Controlling confounding and random assignment

Observational studies

Lecture material. Treatment assignment may depend on patient choice, clinician discretion or disease severity rather than chance. Age, gender, socioeconomic status, family background and housing type are examples of potential confounders; each is a candidate, not automatically a confounder in every study.

Stratification lets us compare outcomes at a fixed level of a measured variable, as with stone size. However, several confounders can operate simultaneously, and some may be unmeasured or unidentified (lurking variables). We cannot assume all confounding has been removed. An observational association alone is not definitive causal evidence.

Randomised controlled trials (RCTs)

Lecture material. Random assignment places patients into X or Y using chance, rather than allowing baseline characteristics to determine treatment. It addresses known as well as unknown or unmeasured confounders and supports stronger causal inference when the trial is well conducted.

Supplementary correction: balance is expected, not guaranteed

The notes write $P(L\mid X)=P(L\mid Y)$. This expresses the ideal of treatment being independent of baseline stone size. Ordinary random assignment does not guarantee exact equality of the observed proportions in one trial. Chance imbalances remain possible; larger groups tend to be more similar.

If both groups actually had the same large-stone share—for example, 75.1%—stone size would no longer be associated with treatment in that comparison. Both treatments would have the same case-mix weights, so the reversal driven by unequal stone-size distributions would disappear.

ApproachWhat it addressesLimitation
Stratify observational dataCompare within levels of a measured potential confounderOther measured or unmeasured confounders may remain
Random assignment before treatmentBalances baseline characteristics in expectation, including unknown onesNot exact balance in every sample; trial conduct still matters

Randomisation is not always feasible, ethical or practical, such as assigning people to smoke or to unjustifiably risky procedures.

12. Exam workflow

  1. Identify the variables and categories. State which group is exposed and which is unexposed.
  2. Identify the study design. Cohort starts from exposure; case-control starts from outcome.
  3. Choose the denominator. Grand total for marginal/joint proportions; subgroup total for conditional proportions.
  4. Choose the measure. RR requires meaningful incidence proportions; OR works for case-control data.
  5. Fix the comparison direction. Write “exposed divided by unexposed” before substituting values.
  6. Interpret in context. Name the groups, outcome and multiplier, then state the direction of association.
  7. Check for confounding. Compare overall and within-stratum rates, inspect subgroup weights, and test whether the third variable is associated with both treatment and outcome.
  8. Avoid causal language unless the study design and evidence justify it.

13. Common pitfalls

  • Comparing counts when group sizes differ instead of comparing conditional proportions.
  • Confusing $P(A\mid B)$ with $P(B\mid A)$.
  • Forgetting that the condition after $\mid$ determines the denominator.
  • Inverting RR or OR. Reversing the numerator and denominator changes the value to its reciprocal and reverses the interpretation.
  • Calling odds a probability, or interpreting an odds ratio as a risk ratio.
  • Calculating RR from a case-control sample.
  • Concluding causation from an observational association, even after controlling for one confounder.
  • Using an unweighted average of subgroup rates when subgroup sizes differ.
  • Assuming random assignment guarantees exactly equal observed confounder proportions.

One-line memory aid

Proportion: part over the relevant whole. Risk: outcome over all people at risk. Odds: outcome over no outcome. Ratio: exposed measure over unexposed measure.
