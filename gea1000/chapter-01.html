Foundations of Quantitative Reasoning with Data

Primary source: Chapter 1 lecture videos. This guide organises the material around the decisions you must make when reading or designing a study.

1. The big picture

A study begins with a question about a population, obtains data from a sample, analyses variables and uses the results to answer or refine the question.

StageMain questionKey ideas
ProblemWhat do we want to learn, and about whom?Research question, target population
PlanHow will units be selected and variables measured?Sampling frame, sampling method, study design
DataWhat was actually recorded?Individuals, categorical and numerical variables
AnalysisWhat patterns appear?Tables, plots, association and correlation
ConclusionWhat can the evidence justify?Generalisability, association, causation

Exploratory data analysis is iterative: patterns in a visualisation or analysis often generate new questions, which lead to further analysis.

2. Population and research questions

The population is the entire group of individuals or objects about which we want to draw a conclusion. A good research question identifies a characteristic or relationship in a clearly defined population.

Research questionTarget populationQuestion type
What percentage of Singapore adults own a car?Singapore adultsEstimate a population proportion
Is the average university course load greater than 20 units?University students in the defined settingTest a population claim
Do female students have a higher GPA than male students at University X?Students at University XCompare subpopulations
Is weekly social-media time related to GPA?The stated student populationInvestigate a relationship

Do not infer a cause from a headline

A fall in Singapore marriages during 2020 does not by itself prove that COVID-19 caused the fall. Ask how the count was obtained, whether population size changed, which other explanations are plausible and whether the design can support causality.

TermMeaningExample

Census versus sample

A census attempts to collect data from every unit in the population. A sample is usually cheaper and faster. A census may be impractical when the population is extremely large or effectively infinite, measurement is destructive, or reaching every unit is too costly or inconvenient.

Coverage of the sampling frame

  • Undercoverage: eligible members of the target population are missing from the frame.
  • Overcoverage: the frame contains ineligible or out-of-scope units.
  • Duplicate coverage: some units appear more than once and may have an excessive chance of selection.

A strong sampling frame closely matches the target population: it covers all eligible units once and excludes unwanted units.

4. Bias and generalisability

Bias is a systematic tendency for a method to favour particular outcomes. Increasing sample size reduces chance variability, but it does not repair a systematically biased design.

ProblemHow it arisesWhy it matters
Selection biasThe selection process systematically favours some units; for example, an imperfect frame or convenience sampleThe sample may differ systematically from the target population
Non-response biasSelected units do not participate or disclose information, and respondents differ from non-respondentsThe observed responses may not represent all selected units
Measurement errorQuestions, instruments, memory or recording procedures produce inaccurate valuesEven a representative sample can yield inaccurate data

When can results be generalised?

Look for a sampling frame that adequately covers the target population, probability-based selection, a sufficiently large sample, low or well-managed non-response, and accurate measurement. Generalisability depends on the entire data-collection process, not sample size alone.

5. Probability sampling

In probability sampling, selection uses a known random mechanism. Selection probabilities need not be equal, but the chance mechanism must be defined. Random selection helps control selection bias; it does not guarantee that a particular sample perfectly represents the population.

MethodHow it worksStrengthMain caution
Simple random sample (SRS)Select units without replacement so every possible sample of the given size has equal chanceSimple probability basis and good long-run propertiesNeeds an accessible sampling frame; non-response can remain
Systematic sampleChoose a random start, then every kth unitEasy to implementA periodic ordering aligned with k can bias representation
Stratified random sampleSplit into strata and randomly sample within every stratumEnsures representation of important subgroups and can improve precisionRequires correct stratum information and a suitable frame
Cluster sampleSplit into clusters, randomly choose clusters, then observe all units in selected clustersReduces travel, time and costSimilar units within a cluster provide less independent information, increasing variability

Strata and clusters are opposites in structure

Within each groupAcross groupsWhat is sampled?
Stratified samplingRelatively homogeneousDifferent from one anotherUnits from every stratum
Cluster samplingIdeally mixed / heterogeneousClusters resemble one anotherA selection of whole clusters

6. Non-probability sampling

Non-probability sampling does not use a known chance mechanism, so selection probabilities are unknown.

  • Convenience sampling: selects nearby or readily available people, such as shoppers at one mall.
  • Volunteer sampling: participants opt in, such as respondents to an open online poll.
  • Quota sampling: fills preset category quotas without random selection within categories.
  • Judgment sampling: researchers choose units they consider suitable.

These approaches may be quick, but selection and non-response biases make population generalisation difficult. A large volunteer poll can still be badly biased.

7. Types and roles of variables

A variable is an attribute that can be measured or labelled for each individual. A dataset contains observations (usually rows) and variables (usually columns).

FamilySubtypeRecognition ruleExample
CategoricalNominalLabels with no natural orderPet type: dog, cat, bird
OrdinalOrdered labels, but gaps are not necessarily equalLikert response: disagree, neutral, agree
NumericalDiscreteNumerical values with gaps, often countsNumber of household members
ContinuousCan meaningfully take any value in an intervalHeight, weight, time

Numbers can still be category labels

Coding “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree” as 1 to 5 does not make the responses numerical. For ordinal data, the order is meaningful, but the difference between 1 and 2 need not equal the difference between 4 and 5.

Independent and dependent variables

The independent variable is the explanatory variable that is manipulated in an experiment or treated as the potential predictor/exposure. The dependent variable is the response or outcome hypothesised to change.

For “Does daily caffeine consumption affect sleep quality among Singaporean adults?”, caffeine consumption is the independent variable and sleep quality is the dependent variable.

Variable type determines which summaries and plots make sense: bar plots for categorical variables, histograms and box plots for numerical distributions, and scatter plots for relationships between two numerical variables.

8. Experiments, controls and random assignment

An experiment deliberately manipulates an independent variable to investigate its effect on a dependent variable. A well-designed randomised experiment can provide evidence of a cause-and-effect relationship.

  • The treatment group receives the intervention of interest.
  • The control group provides a baseline. It may receive no treatment, standard treatment or a placebo.

A high success rate in the treatment group is not enough by itself. If both treatment and control groups have the same success rate, the treatment has not shown an improvement over the baseline.

Confounding

A confounder is related to both the explanatory variable and the outcome, creating an alternative explanation for the observed relationship. If coffee drinkers revise longer than non-coffee drinkers, revision time could explain a difference in exam performance.

Random assignment

Random assignment uses an impartial chance mechanism to place experimental subjects into treatment groups. With sufficiently large groups, it tends to balance both known and unknown confounders, making groups comparable before treatment.

Random sampling and random assignment solve different problems

Random sampling concerns who enters the study and supports generalisation to a population. Random assignment concerns which treatment participants receive and supports causal comparison between groups.

9. Placebos and blinding

A placebo resembles the treatment but contains no active component. The placebo effect is a response caused by participants’ expectations rather than the treatment’s active mechanism.

Design featureWho is unaware?Bias reduced
Subject blindingParticipants do not know their assigned groupExpectation-driven changes in behaviour or reported outcomes
Assessor blindingOutcome evaluators do not know assignmentsResearcher or grader expectations affecting measurement
Double-blind experimentBoth subjects and assessorsBoth sources above

Blinding is distinct from random assignment: assignment makes groups comparable, while blinding reduces behaviour and measurement changes caused by knowing the assignment.

10. Observational studies

An observational study measures exposures and outcomes without assigning the exposure. It is used when manipulation is unethical or impractical, such as studying long-term smoking effects.

DesignStart withTime directionTypical question
Cohort studyExposed and unexposed individuals, initially free of the outcomeFollow forward to outcomesWho later develops the disease?
Case-control studyCases with the outcome and controls without itLook backward for exposureWho was previously exposed?

Because exposure is not randomly assigned, exposed and unexposed groups may differ in other ways. Observational studies can show association, but the association may be confounded and does not by itself establish causation.

12. Exam workflow

  1. State the target population. Who or what is the conclusion about?
  2. Identify the sampling frame and sample. Who could be selected, and who was observed?
  3. Classify each variable. Categorical or numerical; then subtype and role.
  4. Identify selection. Was a probability-sampling mechanism used?
  5. Identify assignment. Did researchers randomly assign exposure?
  6. Check bias and confounding. Consider coverage, non-response, measurement and group differences.
  7. Match the conclusion to the design. Separate sample description, population generalisation, association and causation.

13. Common pitfalls

  • Treating “random” as haphazard rather than a defined chance mechanism.
  • Confusing random sampling with random assignment.
  • Assuming a large sample automatically removes selection bias.
  • Assuming a numerical category code makes a variable numerical.
  • Mixing up stratified sampling, which samples from every stratum, and cluster sampling, which selects whole clusters.
  • Assuming a census is automatically accurate; non-response and measurement error can still occur.
  • Claiming causation from an observational study.
  • Claiming population generalisability from a convenience or volunteer sample.

One-line memory aid

Selection decides whom you may generalise to; assignment decides whether you may argue causation.
