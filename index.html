Section 1

Limits, continuity, and the derivative

A limit lim(x -> a) f(x) = L describes the values of f(x) for x close to a; it does not require f(a) to be defined or equal to L. A two-sided limit exists exactly when both one-sided limits exist and are equal. Continuity at a requires f(a) to be defined, lim(x -> a) f(x) to exist, and that limit to equal f(a).

The derivative at x0 is f'(x0) = lim(Delta x -> 0) [f(x0 + Delta x) - f(x0)] / Delta x, when this limit exists. Geometrically it is the tangent-line slope, obtained as the limit of secant slopes; physically it is an instantaneous rate of change. Never substitute Delta x = 0 before simplifying the difference quotient: that creates 0/0, not a derivative.

For a limit, first try direct substitution. If it gives 0/0, use algebra such as factoring/cancellation or rationalisation, or a justified standard limit. Check left and right limits separately for piecewise formulae, absolute values, or domain boundaries. Trigonometric limits involving sin x / x require radians. L'Hopital's rule belongs later in the course and applies only after the expression has been verified to be an allowed indeterminate quotient form.

  • Tangent at (x0,f(x0)): y - f(x0) = f'(x0)(x-x0).
  • A removable discontinuity has matching one-sided limits but a missing or mismatched function value.
  • Derivative notation: f'(x), df/dx, and Df all denote the derivative when their evaluation point is clear.

Section 2

Differentiation and local analysis

Differentiation rules compress the limit definition into reliable procedures: linearity, the power rule, product rule, quotient rule, and chain rule. The chain rule is the key recognition pattern for a composite expression: differentiate the outer function at the inner expression, then multiply by the derivative of the inner expression. A missing inner derivative is a common exam error.

Use f'(x) to locate critical points: points in the domain where f'(x)=0 or f' does not exist. A sign chart for f' determines increasing and decreasing intervals. Use f''(x) to analyse concavity; an inflection point requires a change in concavity, not merely f''(x)=0. For an absolute maximum/minimum on a closed interval, compare all interior critical points and both endpoints.

Linearisation near a basepoint a is L(x)=f(a)+f'(a)(x-a). Its reliability is local: it normally degrades as x moves away from a. The quadratic approximation adds (f''(a)/2)(x-a)2. Mean Value Theorem arguments require continuity on [a,b] and differentiability on (a,b), and then guarantee a c in (a,b) with f'(c)=[f(b)-f(a)]/(b-a).

Section 3

Modelling with derivatives

Optimisation begins with modelling, not differentiation: define variables, translate constraints, reduce the objective to one variable, identify its feasible domain, then differentiate and justify the final choice. A stationary point is only a candidate; the constraint domain and endpoints determine whether the result is an absolute optimum.

In related-rates problems, write one equation relating the changing quantities, differentiate it with respect to time before inserting the instantaneous numerical values, then substitute the given values and units. This preserves the relationship among all rates. Newton's method solves f(x)=0 iteratively by xn+1=xn-f(xn)/f'(xn), provided f'(xn) is nonzero. It is an approximation method, not a proof that convergence will occur or that it will select the intended root.

For separable differential equations, first rearrange into a y-only expression times dy equal to an x-only expression times dx, integrate both sides, and impose any initial condition. Check domain restrictions introduced by division, logarithms, or inverse operations.

Section 4

Definite integrals and the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus

A definite integral is the limiting accumulated quantity represented by Riemann sums. It can model signed area, accumulated change, mass, work, and other quantities, depending on the integrand and differential. The value is signed: regions below the x-axis contribute negatively to an integral with respect to x.

FTC 1 connects accumulation to differentiation: for a continuous integrand f, an accumulation function F(x)=integral(a to x) f(t) dt has derivative F'(x)=f(x). FTC 2 evaluates a definite integral: if A'(x)=f(x), then integral(a to b) f(x) dx=A(b)-A(a). State the continuity/antiderivative hypotheses before applying these results.

For substitution in an integral, identify an inner expression u and its differential du. Rewrite the entire integrand and bounds in the new variable for a definite integral, integrate with respect to u, then either use transformed bounds or correctly return to x. Do not mix x-bounds with a u-integrand.

Section 5

Applications and numerical integration

Choose a differential element before setting up an application. For volumes of revolution, disks/washers use cross-sectional areas perpendicular to the axis, while shells use circumference times height times thickness parallel to the axis. Sketch the region and axis first; the choice of dx versus dy follows the chosen slices, not a memorised preference.

For work, integrate force with respect to displacement; for average value over [a,b], use (1/(b-a)) integral(a to b) f(x) dx. In probability contexts, a density must be nonnegative and integrate to 1 over its support. Interpret units: integrating a rate with respect to time produces the accumulated quantity.

When an antiderivative is unavailable or data are discrete, approximate the integral. Riemann sums use rectangles, the trapezoidal rule uses linear joins, and Simpson's rule uses quadratic fitting. Simpson's rule requires an even number of subintervals. Numerical answers are approximations and should be labelled as such.

Section 6

Integration-technique selection

Technique selection is structural. Use substitution when the integrand contains a composite function together with its derivative up to a constant factor. Use integration by parts, integral u dv = uv - integral v du, when differentiating one factor simplifies it and integrating the other is feasible. Derive the sign from the product rule rather than memorising an altered formula.

For rational functions, first perform polynomial division if the numerator degree is at least the denominator degree, factor the denominator as far as allowed, decompose into partial fractions, solve coefficients, and integrate each term. The cover-up shortcut works only for the relevant simple linear factors; repeated or irreducible quadratic factors require the correct general numerator form.

For powers of sine and cosine, inspect parity. If one power is odd, save one factor and convert the rest with sin2 x + cos2 x = 1 before substitution. If both are even, use double-angle/half-angle identities. Trigonometric substitutions and inverse-substitution methods require careful domain/triangle choices and a final back-substitution check.

Section 7

Parametric curves, polar coordinates, and improper integrals

For a parametric curve x=x(t), y=y(t), the slope is dy/dx=(dy/dt)/(dx/dt) when dx/dt is nonzero. Compute arc length by integrating ds=sqrt((dx/dt)2+(dy/dt)2) dt over the parameter interval. A parameter direction matters: it determines traversal and can expose repeated tracing.

Polar conversion uses x=r cos theta, y=r sin theta, and r2=x2+y2. Area in polar coordinates is (1/2) integral r2 dtheta over an interval that traces the region exactly once. Draw or analyse the curve to choose bounds; a negative r can represent a point in the opposite direction.

An improper integral is defined by a limit: either a bound is infinite or the integrand is unbounded at an endpoint/interior point. Split at every singularity and test each resulting limit. A finite-looking antiderivative evaluation is invalid unless those defining limits converge. For p-integrals at infinity, integral(1 to infinity) 1/xp dx converges exactly when p>1.

Section 8

Indeterminate forms, sequences, and Taylor series

L'Hopital's rule is a conditional theorem for quotient limits of genuine 0/0 or infinity/infinity form. Verify the form first, differentiate numerator and denominator, and re-evaluate the resulting limit; repeat only while the hypotheses and form justify it. Other indeterminate forms such as 0 times infinity or 1infinity must first be transformed to a valid quotient form. Prefer algebra or local approximations when they expose the result more directly.

For an infinite series sum an, the necessary term test is an -> 0; if terms do not tend to zero, the series diverges. Passing this test does not prove convergence. Choose a supported test by structure: geometric, p-series, comparison, ratio, or other stated convergence criteria, and clearly distinguish convergence of a series from convergence of its terms.

Taylor's formula approximates f near a by a polynomial using derivatives at a: f(x) approximately equals the sum from n=0 to N of fn(a)(x-a)n/n!. A Taylor series is an infinite series; using it as an equality needs an appropriate convergence statement. For an approximation, state the centre, degree, and local nature of the estimate.
