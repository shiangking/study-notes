2. Prerequisite vector toolkit

Vectors and displacement

Write a vector as A=<a₁,a₂,a₃>. Its magnitude is

|A| = √(a₁²+a₂²+a₃²).

For points P=(p₁,p₂,p₃) and Q=(q₁,q₂,q₃),

PQ = Q-P = <q₁-p₁,q₂-p₂,q₃-p₃>.

The subtraction order matters. If you reverse it, the direction reverses.

Dot product

A·B=a₁b₁+a₂b₂+a₃b₃=|A||B|cos θ.

  • Angle: cos θ=(A·B)/(|A||B|). Form both vectors from the angle's vertex.
  • Perpendicularity: nonzero vectors are perpendicular exactly when A·B=0.
  • Component along a unit vector û: A·û.
  • Sign: positive means acute, zero means right angle, and negative means obtuse.

Cross product

For A=<a₁,a₂,a₃> and B=<b₁,b₂,b₃>,

A×B=<a₂b₃-a₃b₂, a₃b₁-a₁b₃, a₁b₂-a₂b₁>.

  • A×B is perpendicular to both A and B.
  • |A×B|=|A||B|sin θ, the parallelogram area.
  • The direction follows the right-hand rule.
  • A×B=-(B×A) and A×A=0.

Common trap: the dot product returns a scalar; the cross product returns a vector.

3. Planes from cross products and normals

Plane through three points

Three non-collinear points P₁,P₂,P₃ determine a plane. Use:

  1. Build two directions in the plane: u=P₂-P₁ and v=P₃-P₁.
  2. Find a normal: N=u×v.
  3. Use point-normal form: N·(P-P₁)=0.
  4. Expand and simplify to ax+by+cz=d if required.

The equivalent determinant condition is

det(P-P₁, P₂-P₁, P₃-P₁)=0,

because the three vectors must be coplanar.

Point-normal form

If N=<a,b,c> is normal to the plane and P₀=(x₀,y₀,z₀) lies on it, then

a(x-x₀)+b(y-y₀)+c(z-z₀)=0.

After expansion, the coefficients of x,y,z are the components of a normal vector. Multiplying the complete equation by a nonzero constant gives the same plane.

Parallel and perpendicular tests

  • A direction v is parallel to a plane when v·N=0.
  • A direction v is perpendicular to a plane when v is a scalar multiple of N.
  • Two planes are parallel when their normals are scalar multiples.

Lecture check: for v=<1,2,-1> and plane x+y+3z=5, the normal is N=<1,1,3>. Since v·N=1+2-3=0, v is parallel to the plane.

Common trap: being perpendicular to the normal means being parallel to the plane.

4. Matrices as transformations

A matrix represents a linear transformation. In AX=U, each entry of U is the dot product of one row of A with the column vector X.

Dimension rule

(m×n)(n×p) → (m×p).

The inner dimensions must match. The outer dimensions give the result size.

Entry rule

(AB)ᵢⱼ = (row i of A) · (column j of B).

Matrix multiplication is generally not commutative: AB need not equal BA, and one of them may not even be defined.

Composition order

(AB)X=A(BX). Therefore B acts first and A acts second. Read a product of transformations from right to left.

Identity and rotation

The identity matrix satisfies IX=X. The matrix

R=[[0,-1],[1,0]]

rotates the plane by 90° counterclockwise: Rî=ĵ, Rĵ=-î, and R²=-I.

Common trap: ordinary matrix multiplication is row-by-column, not entry-by-entry.

Source: MIT 18.02 Lecture Notes - Week 2, PDF page 2.

5. Inverse matrices and cofactors

An inverse exists for a square matrix A when there is a matrix A⁻¹ such that

AA⁻¹=A⁻¹A=I.

If AX=U and A is invertible, then X=A⁻¹U. The central theorem is

A is invertible ⇔ det(A) ≠ 0.

Cofactor method

For a small matrix,

A⁻¹ = adj(A)/det(A).

  1. Determinant: calculate det(A). If it is zero, stop: no inverse exists.
  2. Minors: Mᵢⱼ is the determinant left after deleting row i and column j.
  3. Cofactors: Cᵢⱼ=(-1)^(i+j)Mᵢⱼ, using the sign pattern + - + / - + - / + - +.
  4. Transpose: adj(A)=Cᵀ.
  5. Divide: multiply the adjugate by 1/det(A).
  6. Verify: check that AA⁻¹=I.

Frequent errors: forgetting the checkerboard signs, forgetting to transpose the cofactor matrix, or dividing by the wrong determinant.

Source: MIT 18.02 Lecture Notes - Week 2, PDF pages 2-3.

6. Linear systems as plane intersections

A 3×3 system AX=B represents the intersection of three planes.

ConditionHomogeneous AX=0General AX=B
det(A) ≠ 0Only the trivial solution X=0Exactly one solution, X=A⁻¹B
det(A) = 0Infinitely many solutionsNo solution or infinitely many solutions

Homogeneous systems

For AX=0, every plane passes through the origin, so X=0 is always a solution. If det(A)≠0, it is the unique solution. If det(A)=0, there are infinitely many solutions forming a line or a plane.

Geometric meaning of a zero determinant

If the rows of A are the plane normals N₁,N₂,N₃, then det(A)=0 means the normals are coplanar. The parallelepiped they form has zero volume. A direction along the intersection of two nonparallel planes is N₁×N₂.

Important limitation: for a nonhomogeneous system, det(A)=0 does not tell you whether there are no solutions or infinitely many. Use elimination or another consistency test.

7. Parametric lines and planes

Line through a point

A line through Q₀ with direction v is

Q(t)=Q₀+tv.

Through two distinct points Q₀,Q₁, use v=Q₁-Q₀. Then t=0 gives Q₀ and t=1 gives Q₁.

Line-plane intersection algorithm

  1. Write the line as x=x(t), y=y(t), z=z(t).
  2. Substitute all three into ax+by+cz=d.
  3. Solve the resulting scalar equation for t.
  4. Substitute t back into every coordinate.
Equation after substitutionGeometric conclusion
One value of tOne intersection point
An identity, such as 7=7The entire line lies in the plane
A contradiction, such as 11=7The line is parallel to and outside the plane

Worked lecture example

Given Q₀=(-1,2,2) and Q₁=(1,3,-1),

v=Q₁-Q₀=<2,1,-3>,

so Q(t)=(-1+2t, 2+t, 2-3t).

For the plane x+2y+4z=7, substitution gives

(-1+2t)+2(2+t)+4(2-3t)=11-8t=7.

Thus t=1/2, and the intersection is Q(1/2)=(0,5/2,1/2).

8. Parametric curves and the cycloid cusp

A parametric curve specifies coordinates as functions of a parameter: x=x(t), y=y(t), and possibly z=z(t). The parameter can represent time, angle, or another convenient geometric quantity.

Cycloid derivation

For a wheel of radius a rolling without slipping, let θ be the angle turned. The wheel travels horizontal distance aθ. Decomposing the position of a point on the rim gives

x(θ)=a(θ-sin θ),

y(θ)=a(1-cos θ).

Behaviour near the bottom point

Near θ=0,

sin θ ≈ θ-θ³/6,   cos θ ≈ 1-θ²/2.

Hence

x(θ) ≈ aθ³/6,   y(θ) ≈ aθ²/2.

Therefore y/x ≈ 3/θ, whose magnitude grows without bound as θ→0. The curve has a vertical tangent and a cusp at the bottom point.

General tangent method

For a regular point,

dy/dx = (dy/dt)/(dx/dt), provided dx/dt≠0.

If both coordinate derivatives vanish, the quotient gives 0/0 and is inconclusive. Compare the leading Taylor terms of x(t)-x(t₀) and y(t)-y(t₀) instead.

9. High-yield mistakes checklist

  • Form point-to-point vectors in a consistent order.
  • Do not confuse A·B with A×B.
  • Reversing a cross product reverses the normal direction.
  • A direction orthogonal to a plane's normal is parallel to the plane.
  • Check matrix dimensions before multiplying.
  • Read transformation composition from right to left.
  • Do not compute A⁻¹ before confirming det(A)≠0.
  • Transpose the cofactor matrix when forming the adjugate.
  • det(A)=0 does not automatically mean “no solution.”
  • After solving for a line parameter, substitute it back into all coordinates.
  • When dx/dt=dy/dt=0, use leading-order analysis rather than declaring that no tangent exists.
