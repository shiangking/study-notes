2. Vectors: the foundations

2.1 What is a vector?

A vector records magnitude and direction. In coordinates, $\mathbf a=\langle a_1,a_2,a_3\rangle$ means move $a_1$ units along $x$, $a_2$ along $y$, and $a_3$ along $z$. A point describes a location; a vector describes a displacement. They may use the same tuple notation, so use the context carefully.

$\displaystyle \overrightarrow{PQ}=Q-P=\langle q_1-p_1,q_2-p_2,q_3-p_3\rangle$

The order matters: $\overrightarrow{QP}=-\overrightarrow{PQ}$.

2.2 Magnitude and unit vectors

$\displaystyle \lVert\mathbf a\rVert=\sqrt{a_1^2+a_2^2+a_3^2}$

A unit vector has length $1$. To keep the direction of a nonzero vector but change its length to $1$, normalise it:

$\displaystyle \widehat{\mathbf a}=\frac{\mathbf a}{\lVert\mathbf a\rVert},\qquad \mathbf a\ne\mathbf0$

Worked example: displacement and direction

Let $P=(1,-2,3)$ and $Q=(4,2,3)$. Then

$\overrightarrow{PQ}=\langle4-1,2-(-2),3-3\rangle=\langle3,4,0\rangle$.

Its length is $\sqrt{3^2+4^2}=5$, so the unit direction from $P$ to $Q$ is $\langle3/5,4/5,0\rangle$.

3. Dot product, angles and projections

3.1 Formula and geometric meaning

$\displaystyle \mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b=a_1b_1+a_2b_2+a_3b_3=\lVert\mathbf a\rVert\lVert\mathbf b\rVert\cos\theta$

The coordinate formula computes the number; the geometric formula explains it. It measures how strongly one vector points along the other.

Dot productAngleMeaning
$\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b>0$AcuteMostly the same direction
$\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b=0$$90^\circ$Orthogonal (perpendicular), if both are nonzero
$\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b<0$ObtuseMostly opposite directions

For nonzero vectors,

$\displaystyle \theta=\cos^{-1}\!\left(\frac{\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b}{\lVert\mathbf a\rVert\lVert\mathbf b\rVert}\right)$

Worked example: find an angle

Let $\mathbf a=\langle1,1,0\rangle$ and $\mathbf b=\langle1,0,1\rangle$.

$\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b=1$, while $\lVert\mathbf a\rVert=\lVert\mathbf b\rVert=\sqrt2$.

Therefore $\cos\theta=1/(\sqrt2\sqrt2)=1/2$, so $\boxed{\theta=60^\circ}$.

3.2 Scalar component versus vector projection

These are related but not identical:

QuantityFormulaWhat it gives
Scalar component of $\mathbf A$ along $\mathbf u$$\displaystyle \operatorname{comp}_{\mathbf u}\mathbf A=\frac{\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf u}{\lVert\mathbf u\rVert}$Signed length
Vector projection of $\mathbf A$ onto $\mathbf u$$\displaystyle \operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf u}\mathbf A=\frac{\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf u}{\lVert\mathbf u\rVert^2}\mathbf u$The actual vector along $\mathbf u$

If $\mathbf u$ is already a unit vector, the scalar component simplifies to $\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf u$, and the projection becomes $(\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf u)\mathbf u$.

Worked example: component, projection and orthogonal remainder

Let $\mathbf u=\frac1{\sqrt6}\langle1,-1,2\rangle$ and $\mathbf A=\langle3,5,7\rangle$. First check that $\lVert\mathbf u\rVert=1$.

The scalar component is $\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf u=\frac{3-5+14}{\sqrt6}=\frac{12}{\sqrt6}=2\sqrt6$.

The vector projection is $(2\sqrt6)\frac1{\sqrt6}\langle1,-1,2\rangle=\boxed{\langle2,-2,4\rangle}$.

The perpendicular remainder is $\mathbf A_\perp=\mathbf A-\operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf u}\mathbf A=\langle1,7,3\rangle$. Check: $\mathbf A_\perp\cdot\mathbf u=(1-7+6)/\sqrt6=0$.

Common mistake

“Component” usually means a signed scalar, not an always-positive length. A negative answer says the projected part points opposite to the chosen direction. If the question asks for the projection vector, include the direction vector.

4. Determinants: signed area and volume

4.1 Determinant of two 2D vectors

$\displaystyle \det(\mathbf a,\mathbf b)=\begin{vmatrix}a_1&b_1\\a_2&b_2\end{vmatrix}=a_1b_2-a_2b_1$

The absolute value is the parallelogram's geometric area. The sign records orientation:

  • positive: turning from $\mathbf a$ to $\mathbf b$ is counterclockwise;
  • negative: the turn is clockwise;
  • zero: the vectors are parallel, so the parallelogram collapses to zero area.
$\displaystyle \text{parallelogram area}=|\det(\mathbf a,\mathbf b)|,\qquad \text{triangle area}=\frac12|\det(\mathbf a,\mathbf b)|$

Worked example: signed area

For $\mathbf a=\langle3,1\rangle$ and $\mathbf b=\langle1,2\rangle$, $\det(\mathbf a,\mathbf b)=3(2)-1(1)=5$.

The parallelogram area is $5$ and the corresponding triangle area is $5/2$. Reversing the order gives $-5$: same physical area, opposite orientation.

4.2 Why determinant is connected to the cross product

Embed 2D vectors in 3D by giving them zero $z$-components:

$\displaystyle \langle a_1,a_2,0\rangle\times\langle b_1,b_2,0\rangle=\langle0,0,\det(\mathbf a,\mathbf b)\rangle$

So the 2D determinant is exactly the signed $z$-component of the cross product.

4.3 Determinant in 3D: scalar triple product

$\displaystyle \det(\mathbf a,\mathbf b,\mathbf c)=\mathbf a\cdot(\mathbf b\times\mathbf c)$

Its absolute value is the volume of the parallelepiped. A zero determinant means the three vectors are coplanar and create no 3D volume.

Worked example: your Q04.6

$\mathbf A=\langle1,2,3\rangle$, $\mathbf B=\langle-1,-1,2\rangle$, $\mathbf C=\langle0,1,-1\rangle$.

First, $\mathbf B\times\mathbf C=\langle-1,-1,-1\rangle$.

Then $\det(\mathbf A,\mathbf B,\mathbf C)=\mathbf A\cdot(\mathbf B\times\mathbf C)=-1-2-3=\boxed{-6}$.

The physical volume is $|-6|=6$. The minus sign records orientation only.

5. Cross product and planes

5.1 Formula and properties

$\displaystyle \mathbf a\times\mathbf b=\langle a_2b_3-a_3b_2,\ a_3b_1-a_1b_3,\ a_1b_2-a_2b_1\rangle$
  • $\mathbf a\times\mathbf b$ is perpendicular to both input vectors.
  • $\lVert\mathbf a\times\mathbf b\rVert=\lVert\mathbf a\rVert\lVert\mathbf b\rVert\sin\theta$ is the parallelogram area.
  • Direction follows the right-hand rule.
  • $\mathbf a\times\mathbf b=-(\mathbf b\times\mathbf a)$, so order matters.
  • $\mathbf a\times\mathbf b=\mathbf0$ exactly when the vectors are parallel or one is zero.

Worked example: compute and verify a cross product

For $\mathbf a=\langle1,2,3\rangle$ and $\mathbf b=\langle-1,-1,2\rangle$,

$\mathbf a\times\mathbf b=\langle2(2)-3(-1),\ 3(-1)-1(2),\ 1(-1)-2(-1)\rangle=\langle7,-5,1\rangle$.

Verification: $\langle7,-5,1\rangle\cdot\mathbf a=7-10+3=0$ and $\langle7,-5,1\rangle\cdot\mathbf b=-7+5+2=0$.

5.2 Equation of a plane

A plane is determined by a point $P_0=(x_0,y_0,z_0)$ and a nonzero normal $\mathbf n=\langle a,b,c\rangle$:

$\displaystyle \mathbf n\cdot(\mathbf r-\mathbf r_0)=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad a(x-x_0)+b(y-y_0)+c(z-z_0)=0$

For a plane through three non-collinear points $P_1,P_2,P_3$, form $\mathbf u=P_2-P_1$ and $\mathbf v=P_3-P_1$, then use $\mathbf n=\mathbf u\times\mathbf v$.

Worked example: plane through three points

Let $P_1=(1,0,0)$, $P_2=(0,1,0)$ and $P_3=(0,0,1)$.

$\mathbf u=\langle-1,1,0\rangle$, $\mathbf v=\langle-1,0,1\rangle$, and $\mathbf n=\mathbf u\times\mathbf v=\langle1,1,1\rangle$.

Using $P_1$, $(x-1)+y+z=0$, so the plane is $\boxed{x+y+z=1}$.

6. Matrices: what they actually do

6.1 Terminology and dimensions

An $m\times n$ matrix has $m$ rows and $n$ columns. Its entry $a_{ij}$ is in row $i$, column $j$. A matrix can store coefficients, but the more useful viewpoint here is that it represents a linear transformation that moves vectors while preserving addition and scalar multiplication.

$\displaystyle A(\mathbf x+\mathbf y)=A\mathbf x+A\mathbf y,\qquad A(c\mathbf x)=cA\mathbf x$

6.2 Matrix-vector multiplication

If $A$ is $m\times n$, then it accepts an $n$-component vector and returns an $m$-component vector. Each output entry is one row dotted with the input:

$\displaystyle A\mathbf x=\begin{bmatrix}\text{row 1}\cdot\mathbf x\\\text{row 2}\cdot\mathbf x\\\vdots\\\text{row }m\cdot\mathbf x\end{bmatrix}$

Worked example: applying a transformation

Let $A=\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\-1&3\end{bmatrix}$ and $\mathbf x=\begin{bmatrix}4\\2\end{bmatrix}$.

$A\mathbf x=\begin{bmatrix}2(4)+1(2)\\-1(4)+3(2)\end{bmatrix}=\boxed{\begin{bmatrix}10\\2\end{bmatrix}}$.

The first row asks for $2x+y$; the second asks for $-x+3y$.

6.3 The columns tell you where basis vectors go

For $A=\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{bmatrix}$,

$\displaystyle A\mathbf e_1=\begin{bmatrix}a\\c\end{bmatrix},\qquad A\mathbf e_2=\begin{bmatrix}b\\d\end{bmatrix}$

Therefore $A\begin{bmatrix}x\\y\end{bmatrix}=x(A\mathbf e_1)+y(A\mathbf e_2)$. Once you know where the basis vectors go, linearity determines where every vector goes.

Rotation example from the lecture

$R=\begin{bmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{bmatrix}$ sends $\mathbf e_1$ right-to-up and $\mathbf e_2$ up-to-left: a $90^\circ$ counterclockwise rotation. Applying it twice gives $R^2=-I$, a $180^\circ$ rotation.

7. Matrix multiplication and composition

7.1 Dimension rule

$\displaystyle (m\times n)(n\times p)\longrightarrow(m\times p)$

The inner dimensions must match; the outer dimensions give the result. Entry $(i,j)$ is

$\displaystyle (AB)_{ij}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{ik}b_{kj}$

In plain language: take row $i$ of $A$ dotted with column $j$ of $B$.

Worked example: multiply two matrices

Let $A=\begin{bmatrix}1&2\\3&4\end{bmatrix}$ and $B=\begin{bmatrix}2&0\\1&-1\end{bmatrix}$.

$AB=\begin{bmatrix}1(2)+2(1)&1(0)+2(-1)\\3(2)+4(1)&3(0)+4(-1)\end{bmatrix}=\boxed{\begin{bmatrix}4&-2\\10&-4\end{bmatrix}}$.

But $BA=\begin{bmatrix}2&4\\-2&-2\end{bmatrix}\ne AB$. Matrix multiplication is generally not commutative.

7.2 Why order is reversed for transformations

$\displaystyle (AB)\mathbf x=A(B\mathbf x)$

$B$ touches the vector first, then $A$. So read a product of transformations from right to left. Changing the order can change the final geometry.

Common mistakes

  • Multiplying corresponding entries instead of row-by-column.
  • Checking the outer dimensions instead of the inner dimensions.
  • Assuming $AB=BA$.
  • Reading $AB$ as “do $A$, then $B$”; it is the reverse when acting on vectors.

8. Matrix determinant and invertibility

8.1 Formulas

For a $2\times2$ matrix,

$\displaystyle \det\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{bmatrix}=ad-bc$

For a $3\times3$ matrix, expand along any row or column. Expanding along the first row:

$\displaystyle \det A=a_{11}M_{11}-a_{12}M_{12}+a_{13}M_{13}$

Here $M_{ij}$ is the $2\times2$ determinant left after deleting row $i$ and column $j$. The signs follow the checkerboard $\begin{bmatrix}+&-&+\\-&+&-\\+&-&+\end{bmatrix}$.

8.2 Geometric meaning

$|\det A|$ is the factor by which the transformation scales area in 2D or volume in 3D. The sign tells whether orientation is preserved or reversed. If $\det A=0$, the transformation squashes space into a lower dimension, so information is lost and the transformation cannot be undone.

$\displaystyle A\text{ is invertible}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\det A\ne0$

9. Inverse matrices

9.1 Definition

The inverse $A^{-1}$ undoes $A$:

$\displaystyle AA^{-1}=A^{-1}A=I$

Only square matrices with nonzero determinant have a two-sided inverse. For a $2\times2$ matrix,

$\displaystyle \begin{bmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{bmatrix}^{-1}=\frac1{ad-bc}\begin{bmatrix}d&-b\\-c&a\end{bmatrix},\qquad ad-bc\ne0$

Worked example: inverse and solving a system

Let $A=\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\5&3\end{bmatrix}$. Since $\det A=2(3)-1(5)=1$, $A^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}3&-1\\-5&2\end{bmatrix}$.

To solve $A\mathbf x=\begin{bmatrix}1\\4\end{bmatrix}$, multiply by $A^{-1}$:

$\mathbf x=A^{-1}\mathbf b=\begin{bmatrix}3(1)-1(4)\\-5(1)+2(4)\end{bmatrix}=\boxed{\begin{bmatrix}-1\\3\end{bmatrix}}$.

Check: $A\begin{bmatrix}-1\\3\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1\\4\end{bmatrix}$.

9.2 Cofactor method for $3\times3$ matrices

$\displaystyle A^{-1}=\frac1{\det A}\operatorname{adj}(A)$
  1. Compute $\det A$. If it is zero, stop.
  2. Compute each minor $M_{ij}$.
  3. Apply $C_{ij}=(-1)^{i+j}M_{ij}$ to form the cofactor matrix $C$.
  4. Transpose: $\operatorname{adj}(A)=C^T$.
  5. Divide every entry by $\det A$.
  6. Verify with $AA^{-1}=I$.

Lecture matrix

For $A=\begin{bmatrix}2&3&3\\2&4&5\\1&1&2\end{bmatrix}$, the notes obtain $\det A=3$ and

$A^{-1}=\frac13\begin{bmatrix}3&-3&3\\1&1&-4\\-2&1&2\end{bmatrix}$.

The high-risk steps are the checkerboard signs and the transpose. “Cofactor matrix” and “adjugate” are not the same matrix unless the cofactor matrix happens to be symmetric.

10. Linear systems as geometry

Each equation in a three-variable linear system describes a plane. Solving $A\mathbf x=\mathbf b$ means finding the common intersection.

Condition$A\mathbf x=\mathbf0$$A\mathbf x=\mathbf b$
$\det A\ne0$Only $\mathbf x=\mathbf0$Exactly one solution, $\mathbf x=A^{-1}\mathbf b$
$\det A=0$Infinitely many solutionsNo solution or infinitely many solutions

A homogeneous system always has the trivial solution $\mathbf0$. For a nonhomogeneous system, $\det A=0$ alone cannot distinguish “none” from “infinitely many”; elimination is needed to test consistency.

11. Parametric lines and plane intersections

$\displaystyle \mathbf r(t)=\mathbf r_0+t\mathbf v$

Through distinct points $Q_0,Q_1$, take $\mathbf v=Q_1-Q_0$. For a line-plane intersection, substitute $x(t),y(t),z(t)$ into the plane equation and solve for $t$.

Worked lecture example

For $Q_0=(-1,2,2)$ and $Q_1=(1,3,-1)$, $\mathbf v=\langle2,1,-3\rangle$, so $\mathbf r(t)=\langle-1+2t,2+t,2-3t\rangle$.

Substitute into $x+2y+4z=7$: $11-8t=7$, hence $t=1/2$ and the intersection is $\boxed{(0,5/2,1/2)}$.

After substitutionMeaning
One value of $t$One intersection point
Identity such as $7=7$Entire line lies in the plane
Contradiction such as $11=7$Line is parallel to, but outside, the plane

12. Parametric curves and the cycloid

A parametric curve gives coordinates as functions of a parameter. For a wheel of radius $a$ rolling without slipping, a point on the rim follows

$\displaystyle x(\theta)=a(\theta-\sin\theta),\qquad y(\theta)=a(1-\cos\theta)$

At a regular point, $\displaystyle \frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{dy/dt}{dx/dt}$ when $dx/dt\ne0$. Near $\theta=0$, $x\sim a\theta^3/6$ and $y\sim a\theta^2/2$, so the cycloid has a vertical tangent and a cusp.

13. What to remember for the exam

  • Dot product: angle, orthogonality, component and projection.
  • Cross product: perpendicular vector and parallelogram area; reversing order flips the sign.
  • Determinant: signed area/volume; absolute value gives physical size.
  • Plane: find a normal, then use $\mathbf n\cdot(\mathbf r-\mathbf r_0)=0$.
  • Matrix product: row-by-column; $(AB)\mathbf x=A(B\mathbf x)$, so $B$ acts first.
  • Inverse: exists exactly when $\det A\ne0$; verify by multiplication.
  • $\det A=0$ means a nonhomogeneous system has no solution or infinitely many, not automatically “no solution.”
  • Always state whether your answer is a scalar, vector, area or volume.

14. Quick practice

  1. Find the scalar component and vector projection of $\mathbf a=\langle2,-1,3\rangle$ along $\mathbf u=\langle1,2,2\rangle$.
  2. Find the area of the triangle with vertices $(0,0)$, $(4,1)$ and $(1,3)$.
  3. Find a plane through $(1,1,0)$, $(0,2,1)$ and $(2,0,1)$.
  4. Compute $AB$ and $BA$ for $A=\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{bmatrix}$ and $B=\begin{bmatrix}2&0\\0&3\end{bmatrix}$.
  5. Solve $\begin{bmatrix}3&1\\2&1\end{bmatrix}\mathbf x=\begin{bmatrix}7\\5\end{bmatrix}$ using an inverse.
Practice answers
  1. $\lVert\mathbf u\rVert=3$, $\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf u=6$. Component $=2$; projection $=\frac23\langle1,2,2\rangle=\langle2/3,4/3,4/3\rangle$.
  2. $\frac12|4(3)-1(1)|=11/2$.
  3. A normal is $\langle1,1,0\rangle$, so $x+y=2$.
  4. $AB=\begin{bmatrix}2&3\\0&3\end{bmatrix}$, $BA=\begin{bmatrix}2&2\\0&3\end{bmatrix}$.
  5. $\mathbf x=\langle2,1\rangle$.
