Elementary Number Theory and Methods of Proof

A proof is a logically valid argument showing that a mathematical statement follows from definitions, axioms, and previously established results. Good proofs make every chosen object, assumption, and inference explicit.

4.1 Direct proof and counterexample

4.1.1 Core definitions and axiomatic assumptions

We assume the standard algebraic laws and the following properties of equality for all objects A, B, and C:

  • Reflexive: A = A.
  • Symmetric: if A = B, then B = A.
  • Transitive: if A = B and B = C, then A = C.

We also use these integer facts:

  • There is no integer strictly between 0 and 1.
  • ℤ is closed under addition, subtraction, and multiplication.
  • ℤ is not closed under division: 3/2 ∉ ℤ, and division by zero is undefined.

Even and odd integers

  • n is even iff ∃k ∈ ℤ, n = 2k.
  • n is odd iff ∃k ∈ ℤ, n = 2k + 1.

These definitions are proof tools. To prove an expression E is even, rewrite it as 2 × (an integer). To prove it is odd, rewrite it as 2 × (an integer) + 1.

Prime and composite integers

An integer n is prime iff n > 1 and every positive factorization n = rs is trivial:

(r = 1 ∧ s = n) ∨ (r = n ∧ s = 1).

Formally:

(n > 1) ∧ ∀r,s ∈ ℤ⁺ [n = rs → ((r = 1 ∧ s = n) ∨ (r = n ∧ s = 1))].

An equivalent formulation forbids a factorization by integers both greater than 1:

(n > 1) ∧ ∀r,s ∈ ℤ [(r > 1 ∧ s > 1) → rs ≠ n].

An integer n is composite iff n > 1 and

∃r,s ∈ ℤ⁺ [n = rs ∧ 1 < r < n ∧ 1 < s < n].

Important: 1 is neither prime nor composite because both definitions require n > 1.

4.1.2 Proving existential statements

To constructively prove ∃x ∈ D, Q(x), either:

  1. exhibit a concrete witness x in D and verify Q(x); or
  2. give an explicit algorithm that always produces a valid witness.

Example 1: prove there exists an even integer expressible in two ways as a sum of two primes. Choose 10:

10 = 5 + 5 = 3 + 7, and 3, 5, 7 are prime.

Example 2: let r,s ∈ ℤ. Prove there is k ∈ ℤ with 22r + 18s = 2k.

Choose k = 11r + 9s. Closure under multiplication and addition gives k ∈ ℤ, and

2k = 2(11r + 9s) = 22r + 18s.

A witness alone is not enough; explicitly check both domain membership and the required property.

4.1.3 Disproving universal statements

To disprove ∀x ∈ D (P(x) → Q(x)), prove its negation:

∃x ∈ D (P(x) ∧ ¬Q(x)).

Thus a counterexample must satisfy the hypothesis and violate the conclusion.

Example: disprove “for all real a,b, if a² = b², then a = b”. Choose a = 1 and b = −1. Both are real and a² = b² = 1, but a ≠ b.

Common mistake: choosing an x for which P(x) is false does not refute P(x) → Q(x); that conditional is vacuously true.

4.1.4 Proof by exhaustion

If D is finite—or only finitely many members satisfy the hypothesis—check every relevant case.

Example: every even integer n with 4 ≤ n ≤ 26 is the sum of two primes:

4 = 2 + 26 = 3 + 38 = 3 + 5
10 = 5 + 512 = 5 + 714 = 11 + 3
16 = 5 + 1118 = 7 + 1120 = 7 + 13
22 = 5 + 1724 = 5 + 1926 = 7 + 19

The interval and evenness hypothesis reduce the proof to exactly these twelve cases.

4.1.5 Generalizing from the generic particular

To directly prove ∀x ∈ D(P(x) → Q(x)):

  1. Let x be an arbitrary element of D.
  2. Assume P(x).
  3. Use definitions and established facts to derive Q(x).
  4. Because x was arbitrary, universally generalize.

Example: the sum of two even integers is even.

Let m and n be arbitrary even integers. By definition, m = 2a and n = 2b for some a,b ∈ ℤ. Then

m + n = 2a + 2b = 2(a + b).

Since a + b ∈ ℤ by closure, m + n is even.

Uniqueness proofs

To prove ∃!x ∈ X, P(x), prove both:

  1. Existence: ∃x ∈ X, P(x).
  2. Uniqueness: ∀x,y ∈ X ((P(x) ∧ P(y)) → x = y).

Example: every integer has a unique additive inverse.

Let x ∈ ℤ.

  • Existence: choose y = −x. Since integers are closed under subtraction, y ∈ ℤ, and x + y = x − x = 0.
  • Uniqueness: suppose y and z are additive inverses of x. Then x + y = 0 and x + z = 0. Hence x + y = x + z; subtracting x from both sides gives y = z.

4.2 Proofs about rational numbers

4.2.1 Definition

A real number r is rational iff there exist integers a,b such that

r = a/b and b ≠ 0.

A real number that is not rational is irrational. The representation need not be in lowest terms unless a theorem explicitly requires it.

4.2.2–4.2.3 Basic theorems

Theorem: every integer is rational. Let n ∈ ℤ. Then n = n/1, where numerator and denominator are integers and the denominator is nonzero.

Theorem: the sum of two rational numbers is rational.

Let r and s be arbitrary rational numbers. Then r = a/b and s = c/d for integers a,b,c,d with b,d ≠ 0. Therefore

r + s = a/b + c/d = (ad + bc)/(bd).

By integer closure, ad + bc ∈ ℤ and bd ∈ ℤ. Because b,d ≠ 0, bd ≠ 0. Thus r + s has the required rational form.

Corollary: the double of a rational number is rational. A corollary is a result that follows simply from a theorem. Since 2r = r + r, the sum theorem proves the corollary.

4.3 Proofs about divisibility

4.3.1 Definition

For integers n and d, with d ≠ 0, d divides n, written d | n, iff

∃k ∈ ℤ, n = dk.

Examples: 3 | 12 because 12 = 3·4; 5 ∤ 12 because no integer k satisfies 12 = 5k. Under this lecture's convention, the divisor d is nonzero.

4.3.2 Theorem 4.3.1: a positive divisor is no larger

Theorem: for positive integers a and b, if a | b, then a ≤ b.

  1. Let a,b be arbitrary positive integers and suppose a | b.
  2. By divisibility, b = ak for some k ∈ ℤ.
  3. Because a and b are positive, k is positive, so k ≥ 1.
  4. Multiplying 1 ≤ k by positive a gives a ≤ ak = b.

Therefore a | b → a ≤ b for positive integers a,b. ∎

Theorem 4.3.2: divisors of 1

Theorem: the only integer divisors of 1 are 1 and −1.

  1. Let m ∈ ℤ and suppose m | 1. Then 1 = mk for some k ∈ ℤ.
  2. Since mk = 1 > 0, m and k have the same sign.
  3. Case 1: m,k are positive. The previous theorem gives m ≤ 1, while positivity gives m ≥ 1. Hence m = 1.
  4. Case 2: m,k are negative. Then −m and −k are positive and 1 = (−m)(−k). Case 1 gives −m = 1, so m = −1.

Conversely, 1 | 1 and −1 | 1, so the set of divisors is exactly {−1,1}. ∎

Theorem 4.3.3: transitivity of divisibility

Theorem: for all integers a,b,c, if a | b and b | c, then a | c.

  1. Let a,b,c be arbitrary integers satisfying a | b and b | c.
  2. Then b = ar and c = bs for some integers r,s.
  3. Substitute: c = bs = (ar)s = a(rs).
  4. Let k = rs. Closure gives k ∈ ℤ, and c = ak.

By definition, a | c. ∎

Proof-writing lesson: whenever the goal is a | c, work toward the exact definition c = a × (some integer), then name and verify that integer.

4.4 Indirect proof

4.4.1 Proof by contradiction

To prove S by contradiction:

  1. Assume the negation ¬S.
  2. Derive a contradiction, such as R ∧ ¬R, a violation of a known theorem, or an impossible equation.
  3. Conclude that ¬S is false, so S is true.

Theorem: there is no greatest integer.

  1. Suppose for contradiction that there is a greatest integer g, so g ≥ n for every integer n.
  2. Let G = g + 1. Closure under addition gives G ∈ ℤ.
  3. But G > g, contradicting the claim that g is greatest.

Therefore there is no greatest integer. ∎

The lecture also cites the classic contradiction proof that √2 is irrational: assume √2 = a/b in lowest terms, derive that both a and b are even, and contradict lowest terms.

Expanded example: √2 is irrational

  1. Assume √2 = a/b for integers a,b in lowest terms, with b ≠ 0.
  2. Squaring gives 2 = a²/b², hence a² = 2b². Thus a² is even.
  3. Using the proposition below, a is even, so a = 2k.
  4. Substitute: (2k)² = 2b², so b² = 2k². Hence b², and therefore b, is even.
  5. Both a and b are divisible by 2, contradicting that a/b was in lowest terms.

Therefore √2 is irrational. ∎

4.4.2 Proof by contraposition

To prove ∀x ∈ D(P(x) → Q(x)), it is equivalent to prove the contrapositive ∀x ∈ D(¬Q(x) → ¬P(x)):

  1. Let x be arbitrary in D and assume ¬Q(x).
  2. Derive ¬P(x).
  3. Conclude the contrapositive, hence the original statement.

Proposition: for every integer n, if n² is even, then n is even.

Prove the contrapositive: if n is odd, then n² is odd.

  1. Let n be an arbitrary odd integer. Then n = 2k + 1 for some k ∈ ℤ.
  2. n² = (2k + 1)² = 4k² + 4k + 1 = 2(2k² + 2k) + 1.
  3. Let m = 2k² + 2k. Closure gives m ∈ ℤ.
  4. Thus n² = 2m + 1, so n² is odd.

The contrapositive is true, hence if n² is even, n is even. ∎

Choosing a proof method

Goal shapeGood first approach
∃x, P(x)Construct and verify a witness.
∀x(P(x) → Q(x))Direct proof from an arbitrary x satisfying P.
Universal claim seems falseSearch for one counterexample satisfying P and violating Q.
Finite relevant domainExhaustion.
∃!x, P(x)Separate existence and uniqueness.
¬Q gives more usable information than PContraposition.
Negation creates an impossible/extremal situationContradiction.

Contraposition proves a conditional by directly proving an equivalent conditional. Contradiction assumes the negation of the entire target and derives impossibility. They may look similar, but their starting assumptions differ.

Lecture 04 proof checklist

  1. Write the domain and quantify objects correctly: arbitrary for universal claims, specific for existential claims.
  2. Unpack definitions immediately—especially even, odd, rational, prime, composite, and divides.
  3. When introducing an integer expression, justify that it is an integer using closure.
  4. For division, verify the denominator is nonzero.
  5. A counterexample must make the hypothesis true and conclusion false.
  6. For uniqueness, prove existence and “any two solutions are equal”.
  7. End by explicitly linking the derived form back to the definition or theorem being proved.
